FAQ & Details 
To How to Create A Free World
Written By Omen Waker

1.	Question: If nobody gets paid, what is going to motivate people to do the hard, unpleasant, or dirty jobs (like garbage collection, plumbing repairs, or building construction)?

Answer: In a free world, essential and difficult jobs are handled through shared community contribution and reduced-hour volunteering. Because nobody is trapped working 40+ hours a week at a job they hate just to survive, the total labor required to keep society running is shared collectively by everyone. Furthermore, when people only work a fraction of their week to maintain their community, hard jobs become short, shared rotations rather than lifelong life-sentences. As noted in In a FREE World, when people volunteer to keep the world running for free, everybody does their part so everything stays free for everybody. Plus, automation and technology take care of the heavy lifting, leaving human contribution focused on community care and cooperation.

2.	Question: What stops people from abusing the system, being lazy, or just taking everything without contributing anything back?

Answer: In a free society, we don’t need coercion, poverty traps, or wage slavery to keep the world running; instead, we rely on natural human psychology and community accountability.
If someone chooses to refuse to volunteer and simply stay home, their basic human needs—like food and housing—remain entirely free, because no one is ever left to starve or go homeless. However, they are placed on a non-volunteer status, meaning they temporarily lose access to non-essential communal benefits, such as free entertainment, recreational events, and extra luxury goods. 
Human nature itself solves the rest of the problem:
•	The 3 to 6 Month Wall: After 3 to 6 months of doing nothing but staying at home, a person almost always becomes deeply frustrated with isolation and inactivity. They naturally begin looking for ways to get out of the house, which brings them right back to wanting to participate and volunteer.
•	The One-Year Mark: After a full year of staying shut inside, human psychology reaches a point where a person becomes genuinely willing to shovel manure for a living just for the chance to get outside, connect with others, and contribute.
As explored in The Heart of the World and our foundational philosophy, true freedom isn't about doing nothing—it’s about having the unforced choice to belong, cooperate, and add value to your community. When people realize they are valued like real human beings rather than trapped like currency, cooperation naturally replaces competition, and everyone finds their unique role to play in keeping the world alive. 

3.	Question: Who decides what everyone's required volunteer hours are, and who organizes or manages how work gets done without a government or corporate management structure?

Answer: When you remove the massive overhead of the current economic system—such as financial administration, corporate marketing, ad agencies, insurance bureaucracy, and artificial scarcity—the actual labor needed to keep humanity thriving drops dramatically.
Instead of a grueling 40-to-50-hour workweek designed to maximize profit for someone else, a cooperative global society would only require a fraction of that time:
•	The 15-to-20 Hour Standard: Modern automation, technology, and widespread global participation mean that essential societal maintenance could easily be covered by a sustainable 4-day workweek with roughly 4 to 5 hours max per day (or equivalent flexible shifts).For a full time volunteer.
•	True Distribution of Labor: When everyone participates and contributes their fair share instead of a stressed-out percentage of the population carrying the entire weight, the workload becomes incredibly light.
As projected in our foundational philosophy, once human ingenuity is freed from the endless grind of survival and profit, people discover that keeping the world running takes very little time—leaving the rest of life entirely open for community, art, exploration, and enjoyment.

4.	Question: What about global trade, travel, and logistics? If there is no money, how do resources, food, and goods move between different countries, cities, and regions?

Answer: In a free world, logistics and resource distribution shift from a model of profit and competitive trade to a model of cooperative supply and stewardship.
Drawing from the principles of The How and our global network, here is how it works:
•	Need-Based Allocation vs. Profit: Instead of shipping goods based on who can pay the highest price or generate a profit margin, resources, food, and technology move based on where they are genuinely needed and requested.
•	Global Coordination Networks: Regional and international transport networks (like shipping, rail, and freight) are maintained by volunteer logistics teams whose required volunteer hours are dedicated to keeping the pipeline of global abundance flowing smoothly.
•	The Abundance Principle: When manufacturing and supply chains are stripped of artificial scarcity and corporate hoarding, production is matched directly to human consumption. Regions with surplus resources naturally share with regions in need, because cooperation and mutual elevation ("O-lifty-lo") replace competition as our default global state.

5.	Question: What about personal property, land ownership, and housing? If everything is free, can I still own my home, my car, and my personal belongings, or does the government or community take control of everything?

Answer: In a free world, a clear and vital distinction is maintained between private property (land or industrial assets used to control and exploit others) and personal property (the home you live in, the clothes you wear, your car, and your belongings).
Drawing from the principles of The How, here is how personal security and housing are handled:
•	Absolute Protection of Personal Belongings: Everything you use, cherish, and call your own remains fully yours. Your home, your personal items, and your private sanctuary are completely respected and protected. No one will ever take your personal space or belongings.
•	Housing as a Free Human Right: Because living, food, and housing are entirely free, nobody is tied down by mortgages, rent, or property debt. If you want to live in a specific home or move to a new region, housing is allocated based on availability and genuine need through cooperative community coordination.

•	End of Landlordism and Speculation: Land and real estate are no longer treated as commodities to be bought, sold, or hoarded for profit. Instead, living spaces are stewarded collectively, ensuring that every single person has a secure, beautiful place to live without the perpetual stress of housing insecurity.

6.	Question: Does a government still exist, and how are laws or major decisions handled?

Answer: Yes, structured governance can still exist, but its role is completely redefined. A government by any name—whether called The Union for local matters, The Galactic Union for interstellar or macro-scale matters, The Republic, or any other title—can exist strictly to take care of the minuscule, practical tasks governments were originally intended for.
Drawing from the principles of The How, here is how governance operates in a free world:
•	The Local Union: Handles hyper-local community coordination, local maintenance, and regional well-being, focusing entirely on cooperative service rather than control or taxation.
•	The Galactic Union: Manages larger-scale matters, interplanetary or global logistics, broad infrastructure, and deep systemic coordination across regions.
•	True Purpose of Governance: Instead of ruling over people, enforcing artificial laws, or managing currency, these governing bodies handle everything that a government is genuinely and actually needed for—co-creation, shared protection, and keeping the structural pathways of a free society running smoothly.

7.	Question: If there is no money, how are new inventions, scientific research, art, and creative projects funded or encouraged? What drives people to invent or create if there is no profit to be made?

Answer: In a monetary system, creativity and innovation are often choked by paywalls, patents, corporate gatekeepers, and the need to turn a profit. In a free world, invention is liberated because people no longer have to spend their lives chasing survival money.
Drawing from the principles of The How, here is what drives human progress instead:
•	Passion as the Ultimate Driver: When basic needs like food, housing, and education are 100% free, people are free to pursue what they genuinely love. Artists create because they have a story to tell; scientists research because they want to expand human understanding; inventors build because they want to solve real problems.
•	Open-Source Collaboration: Instead of hoarding intellectual property or locking ideas behind corporate patents, innovations are shared instantly across global networks. When one person builds a breakthrough, the whole world benefits immediately through collaborative co-creation.
•	Access to Tools and Resources: Laboratories, maker spaces, digital design tools, and material resources are made universally available to anyone with a vision. You don't need venture capital or a bank loan to build something amazing—you just need your imagination and the cooperative support of your community.

8.	Question: What about health care, medicine, and wellness? Without insurance companies, billing departments, and medical profits, how are doctors, hospitals, and medical research supported?

Answer: In a monetary system, healthcare is often tied to employment, burdened by massive administrative overhead, and driven by industry profits rather than true healing. In a free world, health is treated as a fundamental human right and a core pillar of societal well-being.
Drawing from the principles of The How, here is how medicine and wellness operate:
•	Care Without Financial Barriers: Because survival, housing, and education are free, doctors, nurses, researchers, and holistic practitioners enter the medical field out of a genuine calling to help and heal others, rather than to chase high salaries or pay off massive student loans.
•	Universal Access to Treatment: Hospitals, clinics, and wellness centers are fully supported by the community's cooperative volunteer infrastructure. If someone is sick or injured, they receive immediate, top-tier care without ever having to worry about insurance approvals, medical debt, or billing.
•	Focus on Prevention and Co-Creation: With the elimination of corporate pharmaceutical gatekeeping, medical research shifts toward true preventative wellness, open-source medical technology, and total human flourishing. Healing becomes a collaborative effort supported by the whole community.

9.	Question: What about education and learning? How are schools, universities, and skill training run without tuition fees, student loans, or economic tracking?
Answer: In a monetary system, education is frequently gated behind massive financial debt, used as a tool for career tracking, or restricted by tuition costs. In a free world, learning is treated as a lifelong adventure and a fundamental human offering.
Drawing from the principles of The How, here is how education and skill development operate:
•	100% Free Access to Knowledge: Schools, universities, academies, and digital training platforms are entirely free to access for anyone at any stage of life. There are no student loans, tuition bills, or financial barriers blocking someone from learning what they love.
•	Learning Driven by Passion and Contribution: People study and train because they want to master a craft, explore a science, or prepare for the specific volunteer role they genuinely want to hold in society. Education connects directly to personal growth rather than corporate resume-building.
•	Mentorship and Open Sharing: Experienced educators, mentors, and specialists contribute their required volunteer hours to teaching and guiding the next generation. Knowledge is shared freely, collaboratively, and openly across all communities, ensuring that human wisdom flows without restriction.

10.	Question: What about currency, exchange, and personal trade? If I want to travel, trade a homemade craft with someone, or get something special from another region, how does that work without money?

Answer: In a monetary system, trade and travel are gated by currency exchange, ticket prices, and commercial transactions. In a free world, personal exchange shifts from economic barter to a culture of mutual sharing, hospitality, and gifting.
Drawing from the principles of The How, here is how personal movement and gifting operate:
•	Travel as a Universal Freedom: Transportation systems, trains, flights, and public lodging spaces are maintained through cooperative community contribution, making travel completely open and accessible to anyone who wishes to explore the world.
•	The Gift Economy and Craft Sharing: If you make a handmade craft, bake something special, or want to share your creations with someone else, you simply gift it freely. In return, people share their own skills, art, and creations with you, creating a rich network of appreciation and connection without needing price tags or currency.
•	Access to Special Goods: Regional specialties and unique items circulate through cooperative networks and community distribution centers. Because people create and share out of joy and pride in their work rather than for profit, acquiring special or unique items becomes an act of human connection rather than a commercial transaction.

11.	Question: What if there are way too many people all wanting to go to Hawaii at the same time? Who decides who gets to go?

Answer: When travel is completely free, popular destinations like Hawaii or major cultural events will naturally experience high demand. Without price tags or luxury gatekeeping to artificially restrict who can travel, society handles capacity through fairness, logistics, and transparent organization.
Drawing from the principles of The How, here is how high-demand situations are managed:
•	First-Come, First-Served & Waiting Lists: Just like booking any limited experience, high-demand destinations utilize straightforward, orderly waiting lists and first-come, first-served scheduling. If a location reaches its sustainable ecological or logistical capacity, travelers simply reserve their spot in line and head out when their turn arrives.
•	Equitable Access Over Wealth: In a monetary system, only those with extra disposable income get to vacation in paradise, while everyone else is priced out. In a free world, access isn't determined by how much money you have, meaning anyone has an equal opportunity to experience the world, provided through fair queuing rather than financial privilege.
•	The Reality of Abundance: When transportation, lodging, and hospitality infrastructure are powered by cooperative contribution rather than profit caps, capacity scales up massively to meet human desire. While extreme bottlenecks like a simultaneous rush to a single island are managed by simple waiting lists, a society built on cooperation ensures that adventure, rest, and exploration remain open to everyone over time.

12.	Question: Do companies exist in a free world? Who owns them, and is there company ownership?

Answer: Traditional corporate entities driven by profit margins, shareholder dividends, private equity, and board-level control do not exist in a free world. However, cooperative enterprises, creator guilds, and collective workshops thrive.
Drawing from the principles of The How, here is how productive groups and organizational structures operate:
•	The Death of Private Corporate Ownership: No individual or group can "own" a company as private property or intellectual real estate to extract profit from others. There are no shares to buy or sell, no corporate headquarters dictating top-down mandates, and no CEO earning a multi-million dollar salary off the labor of others.
•	Worker and Community Stewardship: Instead of being owned, productive hubs—whether it's a bakery, a manufacturing collective, a research lab, or a transport network—are stewarded by the people who work in them and the community they serve. Those who participate in running the enterprise guide its direction collaboratively.
•	Organized for Creation, Not Commerce: Groups organize organically to build, create, and solve problems because they share a common passion or a mission to serve. They function as open, collaborative guilds where tools, resources, and outputs flow freely to support the broader society.

13.	Question: What about crime, safety, and justice? If there is no money, no police force driven by the state, and no prison industry, how do you handle wrongdoing, disputes, or people who cause harm in a free society?

Answer: In a monetary system, crime is largely driven by poverty, desperation, artificial scarcity, and the psychological stress of a survival culture. In a free world, while the vast majority of crimes disappear because their root causes are removed, society remains fully prepared for the rare exceptions.
Drawing from the principles of The How, here is how safety, law, and justice operate:
•	Dedicated Safety Volunteers: There will always be people who willingly step up to volunteer as police, investigators, and detectives to protect the public. They ensure that all possible scenarios are covered and that the world remains completely safe.
•	Courts, Law, and Accountability: Courts, laws, and legal standards still exist in a free world. When individuals are truly ill, unwell, or malicious and still choose to cause harm, the law is actively applied to them.
•	Rarely Used, Minimal Footprint: Because systemic desperation is gone, the legal system is barely ever used and requires only minimal volunteers to maintain. However, if formal intervention or jail time is required to protect the public, it is utilized, ensuring that safety, peace, and total security are never compromised.

14.	Question: What about transition? How do we actually get from our current monetary, corporate system to this free world without everything collapsing or descending into chaos during the shift?

Answer: Transitioning from a world built on artificial scarcity, debt, and competition to a cooperative free society doesn't happen overnight through violent upheaval—it happens through a systematic, peaceful shift in how we build and sustain our lives.
Drawing from the principles of The How, here is how the transition unfolds safely and steadily:
•	Building the Parallel Infrastructure First: You don't destroy the old system; you simply render it obsolete by building the new one alongside it. Communities gradually establish free access networks, open-source cooperatives, shared resource hubs, and local coordination bodies (like Local Unions) that meet people's needs directly.
•	Conscious Shift in Language and Mindset: The transition begins in the mind and through communication. By stepping away from the manipulative, stress-based language of the old matrix—and aligning with high-frequency service, gratitude, and truth—people naturally dismantle the psychological hooks of corporate control.
•	Peaceful Graduation to Abundance: As more people choose to participate in cooperative systems, contribute their minimal weekly hours, and share resources freely, the reliance on money naturally fades away. The old monetary structures simply dry up from a lack of participation, allowing humanity to graduate smoothly, safely, and peacefully into total freedom.

Answer: You don't have to wait for a global decree or official permission to start living freely. Stepping into a free world begins with a shift in your daily choices, your mindset, and how you connect with the people around you.
Drawing from the principles of The How, here is how you can begin participating today:
•	Shift Your Focus to Service and Creation: Start focusing your time and energy on what you genuinely love to create, build, or share. Whether it's art, writing, gardening, coding, or simply helping a neighbor, channel your energy into passion rather than survival stress.
•	Practice the Gift Economy Locally: Look for opportunities to share your skills, resources, and creations freely without keeping score. Gift your time, exchange homemade crafts with friends, and help others in your community simply because it feels good to elevate one another.
•	Connect with Like-Minded Communities: Find and plug into collaborative networks, local groups, or online spaces (like cooperative builder servers and community circles) where people are actively discussing, planning, and building parallel structures. By aligning with others who share the vision, you help anchor the new reality right where you are.
